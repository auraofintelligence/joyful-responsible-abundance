The whole-system proposal

Evidence and public decisions

Follow a household bill, a legal question or a local problem through evidence, responsibility and a public response.

On this page

  2. How a concern becomes a public decision
  3. Sharing evidence people can check

Reading a bill and finding the rule

You open a household bill and see a charge you cannot explain. You need the date, the amount, the contract you agreed to and the wording the provider relies on. A question backed by those details is easier to act on than a general complaint. This is a small decision with a useful lesson in how evidence travels.

Household Bill Check is a browser workbench that helps you compare offers on the same terms, add up what a fee costs over a year and prepare a question for your provider. If a contract points to another document, you can note the reference and export the wording and research questions to a file you control. The first decision can be as ordinary as asking for an explanation or choosing whether to switch.

The method comes from Luke’s Relevance. Luke began by gathering the forms, contracts and laws that touched his own life. He read across them, following references from one document to the next. He calls that process the Relevance Ladder. Start with the part of life that matters to you, find the rule behind it, then ask what you can do under that rule or what would need to change. The Your Relevance questionnaire helps another person work out which places, activities and obligations belong in their own search.

The Australian Legal Engine helps with the next step. You give it copies of legal texts to search. It can find and quote an exact section, show its date and follow references to other sections. If you search for “landlord”, the tool may also look for “lessor”, the term used in some legislation, and show you that it did so. The source date matters because a section can change between the day you were charged and the day you look it up. Working out whether it applies also depends on the actual contract and circumstances.

You can finish with a clear question and a source you can show someone else. If several households encounter the same charge, they can decide whether there is enough common evidence to take the issue further.

How a concern becomes a public decision

Someone who wants a safer route to a hall has several questions before they can ask for a change. Who owns or manages the path? Which body can pay for a repair? Who else uses it? When can the affected people see a proposal and respond? The answer might involve the venue, a council or another body. Knowing the responsible institution changes where people put their effort.

P4A the Purple Party for Australia is a proposed civic and political project with a public workbench people can explore. P4A Cinema is the public repository behind that workbench. Its Start your law map page adapts Your Relevance to help people separate the council, ward, state electorate and federal electorate that matter to them. The Redland civic workbench shows how a local issue can be linked to records, people who make decisions, ways to take part and accounts of what changed.

A Twinkle is one entry point. It starts with a short scene about an everyday problem. Someone who recognises it can follow the material underneath to the underlying question, sources and a practical route to participate. A longer-range proposal, the Cyber-Republic Simulator, explores how people could rehearse possible rules and institutions before deciding whether they want to adopt them.

Return to the disputed bill. One household can ask for a remedy. If several households report similar charges, each can choose which details to share. A local group can compare those accounts, describe what it can substantiate and prepare a specific request for a provider, regulator or inquiry with the power to act. It can also record where the cases differ. That gives a decision-maker something concrete to answer.

The public record then has a job after the request is sent. Who acknowledged it? What reason did they give for their decision? Did the remedy happen? When can it be reviewed? The record can show that sequence while a person’s own case stays with them. The next group can use the method, see what the first group learnt and begin with better questions.

Sharing evidence people can check

A council is deciding when to close a coastal road during flooding. One computer model shows water reaching the road early. Another gives people longer to get through. A map can make either forecast look convincing. Residents need to see the tide readings and road heights each team used, the assumptions each made and how earlier forecasts matched what happened.

The proposed Web3 Sensorium would give people a place to do that work together. It extends the idea of a digital twin from a person to a place or system. In this case the model of the road could hold its shape, water observations and different ways of estimating how water moves. People could run both versions using the same observations, change one assumption and see how much difference it makes. They could attach a question to the reading, map or test it concerns.

A result would carry the date and version of the inputs, the model used and the settings that produced it. A digital signature can help show who signed a record and whether it has changed since. To assess the claim, someone still needs to check the measurements and the method. A group in another town could repeat the run and test the model against observations from another storm.

Independently operated computers would keep the tools and records needed for local work. When a connection returns, their operators can exchange approved updates. If someone finds a road height was entered incorrectly, the original result, the correction and the new result stay linked. People who used the first forecast can see what changed.

The proposed P4A public Truth Engine applies a similar habit to public claims. A factual claim can carry its source and date, with a way for people to respond and for a reviewer to record a correction. A forecast, opinion and piece of satire each need different treatment. The Sensorium also gives unusual scientific ideas a way to be examined. State the claim clearly, show what supports it and specify what might change the conclusion. People can disagree about what the evidence means and still give others enough information to check it.

Following one problem from notice to repair

The Verse Box proposal imagines a resident who finds the route to a gathering difficult to use with a mobility aid. Follow that case through the system. The people who use the path explain where the difficulty is. They decide what photographs or descriptions can be shared. A facilitator helps turn those observations into a clear question about access for the next event and a separate question about a lasting improvement.

The hall organiser can change some things for this weekend. A more permanent repair may need approval, funding and a check by someone qualified to assess the design. The public workbench records the question and who is responsible for the next step. A resident can keep a personal record of what they learnt in their Aura. It can travel with them to another issue without carrying the private details of everyone involved.

A shared computer can help people compare a map of the route or a model of where water collects. The people who actually use the path can point out what the map misses. If a measurement or assumption is wrong, the group can correct it and see whether the preferred option changes. Whoever has authority to approve the work can explain the decision and name when the result will be checked.

The three records from the Braided Economy answer different questions about the same work. What was paid for? What time was freely given and verified? What difference did the change make? If a volunteer gives 80 minutes and wants to record it, the contribution is one and a third C-Hours. Paid coordination and materials stay in the financial account. The result is whether people could reach the gathering, and whether the work left the next event easier to organise.

Another place could use the method, make its own observations and improve the explanation. A trainer might use it to teach how to take a useful measurement, find the responsible body or invite a correction. This is the link between personal growth and a network. The person gains a skill. The community gains a record it can use. Other communities gain a method they can test against their own conditions.
